This can reduce sensitivity to pointwise errors and allow using lower regularity trial functions. For example, for the wave equation, you would integrate (∂²u/∂t² – c²∇²u) multiplied by a test function over space (and possibly time), yielding boundary terms and volume terms. For Dirichlet BC (specified displacement/pressure on boundary), sample points on the boundary and include an MSE term between the network’s output and the known boundary values. For Neumann or absorbing BC (specified stress or zero reflection), include terms involving spatial derivatives of $u$ (which you can get via autodifferentiation) and match them to the boundary condition. If using the weak form, some BCs will naturally be included in the weak residual as boundary integrals; however, you might still add explicit loss terms to enforce them strongly. Ensure the PINN training data includes collocation points not only in the domain interior (for the PDE) but also on boundaries (for BCs).

Structure your code clearly: define the neural network model (e.g., a fully-connected network representing u(t,x)), use autograd to compute time and spatial derivatives, and form the residual. # WavePINN in Heterogeneous Media: Weak Form, Boundary Conditions, and JAX Implementation

## 1. Weak-Form Residuals for the 3D Heterogeneous Acoustic Wave Equation

Consider the 3D acoustic wave equation in a heterogeneous medium (wave speed $c(\mathbf{x})$):

$$ \partial\_{tt} u(\mathbf{x},t) - c^2(\mathbf{x}),\nabla^2 u(\mathbf{x},t) = 0, \qquad \mathbf{x}\in \Omega,; t\in\[0,T]. $$

To derive the **weak form**, we multiply the PDE by a test function $v(\mathbf{x},t)$ (assumed sufficiently smooth) and integrate over the space-time domain $\Omega \times \[0,T]$. This gives:

$
\int\_0^T!!\int\_{\Omega} \Big(\partial\_{tt}u(\mathbf{x},t) - c^2(\mathbf{x}),\nabla^2 u(\mathbf{x},t)\Big),v(\mathbf{x},t),d\mathbf{x},dt = 0.
$

We then perform integration by parts on the second-order terms to **lower their derivative order** and expose boundary terms:

- **Time integration by parts:** Integrating $\partial\_{tt}u \cdot v$ by parts in $t$ (assuming $v(\mathbf{x},t)$ vanishes at $t=0$ and $t=T$ for simplicity) moves one time derivative onto $v$. This yields
  $
  \int\_0^T!!\int\_{\Omega} \partial\_{tt}u,v,d\mathbf{x}dt
  \= -\int\_0^T!!\int\_{\Omega} \partial\_{t}u,\partial\_{t}v,d\mathbf{x}dt ;+; \Big\[ \int\_{\Omega} \partial\_{t}u(\mathbf{x},t),v(\mathbf{x},t),d\mathbf{x} \Big]\_{0}^{T}.
  $
  The term in brackets is evaluated at the time boundaries. In a weak formulation, one typically incorporates initial conditions separately, so we can set $v(\mathbf{x},T)=0$ and $v(\mathbf{x},0)=0$ to eliminate those end-time terms (alternatively, they would produce terms involving initial data).

- **Spatial integration by parts:** We rewrite the spatial term as $-c^2\nabla^2 u,v = -\nabla\cdot(c^2\nabla u),v$ (assuming $c(\mathbf{x})$ is smooth) and integrate by parts over $\Omega$. Using the divergence theorem:
  $
  \int\_{\Omega} -c^2\nabla^2 u,v,d\mathbf{x} = \int\_{\Omega} \nabla\cdot(c^2\nabla u),v,d\mathbf{x}
  \= -\int\_{\Omega} c^2 \nabla u \cdot \nabla v,d\mathbf{x} ;+; \int\_{\partial\Omega} c^2 (\nabla u \cdot \mathbf{n}),v,ds,
  $
  where $\mathbf{n}$ is the outward unit normal on the boundary $\partial\Omega$. The volume term now involves first-order spatial derivatives ($\nabla u \cdot \nabla v$), and the **boundary integral** $\int\_{\partial\Omega} c^2 (\nabla u \cdot \mathbf{n}) v,ds$ arises naturally.

Combining these results, the weak form (with no forcing) is: find $u$ such that for all test functions $v$ (with $v=0$ on any Dirichlet boundaries and vanishing at $t=0,T$),

$
\int\_0^T!!\int\_{\Omega} \Big( \partial\_t u,\partial\_t v + c^2(\mathbf{x}),\nabla u \cdot \nabla v \Big),d\mathbf{x}dt ;-; \int\_0^T!!\int\_{\partial\Omega} c^2(\mathbf{x}),(\nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n}),v,ds,dt = 0.
$

This is equivalent to the statement:

$
\int\_{\Omega} (\partial\_{tt}u),v,d\mathbf{x} + \int\_{\Omega} c^2\nabla u \cdot \nabla v,d\mathbf{x} - \int\_{\partial\Omega} c^2 (\nabla u \cdot \mathbf{n}),v,ds = 0, $

holding for all $t\in\[0,T]$. Here the boundary term corresponds to the **Neumann condition**: it will vanish for test functions if $c^2\nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n}=0$ on $\partial\Omega$ (natural Neumann BC), or more generally it equals $\int\_{\Gamma\_N} c^2(\nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n}) v,ds$ which must balance any prescribed flux on $\Gamma\_N$. This weak form clearly **isolates the boundary integral** representing the flux (normal derivative) term【18†L13-L20】. In summary, for our homogeneous wave equation (no source $f$), the weak-form residual statement is:

$$ (v, \partial\_{tt}u)*{\Omega} + c^2 ( \nabla v, \nabla u )*{\Omega} - c^2 (v, \nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n})\_{\partial\Omega} = 0, $$

where $(\cdot,\cdot)*{\Omega}$ and $(\cdot,\cdot)*{\partial\Omega}$ denote volume and boundary inner products, respectively【18†L13-L20】. (Any imposed source term $(f,v)$ would appear on the right-hand side.)

**Neumann boundary term:** If a Neumann boundary condition is specified on some boundary portion $\Gamma\_N \subset \partial\Omega$ (e.g. $c^2,\nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n} = g(\mathbf{x},t)$, a given flux), this term enters the weak form. If a *Dirichlet* condition is enforced on $\Gamma\_D = \partial\Omega \setminus \Gamma\_N$, we usually choose test functions $v$ that vanish on $\Gamma\_D$, so that no boundary term comes from $\Gamma\_D$ (the boundary integral only runs over $\Gamma\_N$). Thus, the weak-form residual naturally incorporates Neumann BCs as boundary integrals, while Dirichlet BCs are handled by the function space constraints (or later, via penalty terms).

For completeness, **initial conditions** $u(\mathbf{x},0)=u\_0(\mathbf{x})$ and $\partial\_t u(\mathbf{x},0)=v\_0(\mathbf{x})$ are not built into the spatial weak form above; they must be satisfied separately (e.g. through additional conditions in the solution space or constraints in the loss, as we will do in the PINN formulation).

## 2. Encoding Mixed Boundary Conditions in the PINN Loss Function

In a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) solution, boundary conditions are enforced by adding appropriate terms to the loss function (or by designing the network to satisfy them identically). We consider **mixed boundary conditions**, where part of the boundary $\Gamma\_D$ has Dirichlet conditions (fixed $u$) and the remainder $\Gamma\_N$ has Neumann conditions (specified normal derivative or flux). Here’s how to encode each:

- **Dirichlet Boundary Conditions (DBC):** For $\mathbf{x}\in \Gamma\_D$, $u(\mathbf{x},t)$ is prescribed (e.g. $u=g\_D(\mathbf{x},t)$). In a PINN, one can enforce this **strongly** or **softly**:
  - *Strong enforcement:* Construct the neural network output $u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)$ to automatically satisfy $u\_{\theta}=g\_D$ on $\Gamma\_D$. For example, if $g\_D$ is a known function (possibly 0), one can design $u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t) = g\_D(\mathbf{x},t) + \phi(\mathbf{x}),N\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)$, where $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is a smooth masking function that vanishes on $\Gamma\_D$ (e.g. distance to $\Gamma\_D$), and $N\_{\theta}$ is the neural network output (unconstrained)【18†L13-L20】. This guarantees $u\_{\theta}$ matches the Dirichlet values by construction.
  - *Soft enforcement (penalty method):* Add a **boundary MSE loss** for Dirichlet points. Sample points ${(\mathbf{x}*i,t\_i)}$ on $\Gamma\_D$ and penalize the error: $L*{DBC} = \frac{1}{N\_D}\sum\_{i} \big(u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\_i,t\_i) - g\_D(\mathbf{x}\_i,t\_i)\big)^2$. This term drives the network to match the prescribed boundary values. This is the simplest approach and is widely used【14†L1-L4】. (In practice, one may weight this loss term to ensure Dirichlet conditions are satisfied to high accuracy.)

- **Neumann Boundary Conditions (NBC):** For $\mathbf{x}\in \Gamma\_N$, a Neumann condition specifies the normal derivative (flux) on the boundary: $\displaystyle \frac{\partial u}{\partial n}(\mathbf{x},t) = g\_N(\mathbf{x},t)$ (or if using flux form, $c^2 \frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=g\_N$). In a PINN, we incorporate this by using **automatic differentiation to compute the normal derivative** of the network output. Thanks to the network’s differentiability, we can obtain $\nabla u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)$ exactly via *backpropagation* (autodiff), which is a key advantage of PINNs【15†L235-L243】. For each sample on $\Gamma\_N$, compute
  $
  (\nabla u\_{\theta} \cdot \mathbf{n})(\mathbf{x}*j,t\_j) = \sum*{k=1}^{3} \frac{\partial u\_{\theta}}{\partial x\_k} n\_k,
  $
  and penalize the difference from the target flux $g\_N(\mathbf{x}*j,t\_j)$. The Neumann loss can be $L*{NBC} = \frac{1}{N\_N}\sum\_{j}\Big((\nabla u\_{\theta}\cdot \mathbf{n})(\mathbf{x}\_j,t\_j) - g\_N(\mathbf{x}*j,t\_j)\Big)^2$. In implementation, one uses `jax.grad` (or equivalent) to get $\nabla u*{\theta}$ at boundary points, and then a dot product with the known normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ for each point. This effectively enforces the boundary integral term from the weak form to match the prescribed flux.

- **Initial Conditions:** Although not a spatial boundary condition, for time-dependent problems we also enforce initial conditions similarly. For initial time $t=0$, we can include losses
  $
  L\_{IC1} = \frac{1}{N\_{I}}\sum\_{m}\big(u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}*m,0) - u\_0(\mathbf{x}*m)\big)^2, \qquad
  L*{IC2} = \frac{1}{N*{I}}\sum\_{m}\Big(\partial\_t u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\_m,0) - v\_0(\mathbf{x}*m)\Big)^2,
  $
  to impose $u(\mathbf{x},0)=u\_0(\mathbf{x})$ and $\partial\_t u(\mathbf{x},0)=v\_0(\mathbf{x})$. As with Neumann BCs, $\partial\_t u*{\theta}$ is obtained via autodiff.

In summary, the PINN loss $\mathcal{L}$ will be a weighted sum of:
$ \mathcal{L} = L\_{\text{physics}} + L\_{DBC} + L\_{NBC} + L\_{IC}, $
where $L\_{\text{physics}}$ penalizes the PDE residual in the domain, and the other terms enforce boundary/initial conditions. By minimizing $\mathcal{L}$, the network is trained to satisfy the PDE **and** all boundary/initial conditions. Modern PINN frameworks and research often emphasize that using automatic differentiation makes it straightforward to incorporate complex BCs in the loss【15†L235-L243】【14†L1-L4】.

*Implementation note:* In the weak-form approach, one might weight the PDE residual by test functions. However, in practice we often minimize the **mean squared residual** (which enforces a weighted $L^2$ norm of the residual). This is equivalent to enforcing the weak form in a least-squares sense. We can use Monte Carlo or quadrature sampling of points in the domain to approximate the volume integrals【9†L249-L257】. The approach below will reflect that by sampling collocation points and minimizing the average squared residual (approximating $\int\_\Omega (\text{residual})^2 d\mathbf{x}$ up to a constant factor).

## 3. JAX + Haiku PINN Code Template for Heterogeneous Wave Equation

Below is a **JAX** + **Haiku** code template that implements a PINN for the 3D wave equation in heterogeneous media. It includes:

- A neural network model (MLP) for $u\_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)$.
- Sampling of collocation points (for the PDE residual in the domain), Dirichlet boundary points, Neumann boundary points, and initial condition points.
- Construction of the physics-informed loss: weak-form volume residual (via sampled integral), Dirichlet BC loss, Neumann BC loss (using gradient for normal derivative), and initial condition losses.
- Use of `jax.grad` and `jax.vmap` for computing derivatives and vectorizing computations across batches of points.

**Note:** This template is **research-ready** but simplified for illustration. It can be extended for complex geometries (by sampling appropriate boundary points) and enhanced with advanced architectures (e.g. Fourier feature embeddings or SIREN activations to capture high-frequency content【6†L65-L72】). Comments in the code explain each key part:

```python
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
import haiku as hk
import optax  # Optimizer library for JAX (optional, for training loop)

# Neural network model definition using Haiku (MLP architecture).
# We define an MLP that takes 4 inputs (x, y, z, t) and outputs u.
def forward_fn(x):
    # x is a vector of shape (4,) representing (x, y, z, t).
    # We use a 5-layer MLP (4 hidden layers of width 64, tanh activation, final linear layer).
    mlp = hk.nets.MLP([64, 64, 64, 64, 1], activation=jax.nn.tanh, activate_final=False)
    return mlp(x)

# Transform the forward function into a Haiku model (with parameters).
model = hk.transform(forward_fn)

# Initialize model parameters.
rng = jax.random.PRNGKey(42)
params = model.init(rng, jnp.zeros((4,)))  # Initialize with a dummy input (4,)

# Define the heterogeneous wave speed c(x). This can be a function or known dataset.
def c_function(x_vec):
    """Wave speed function c(x) for heterogeneous medium."""
    # x_vec is an array of shape (3,) for spatial coords (x,y,z).
    # Example: c could vary with x; here we use a simple constant for illustration.
    return 1.0  # constant speed; replace with spatially varying function as needed

# Sampling points in the domain and on boundaries:
# Define domain bounds (for example, unit cube [0,1]^3 in space and [0,T] in time).
x_min, x_max = 0.0, 1.0
y_min, y_max = 0.0, 1.0
z_min, z_max = 0.0, 1.0
t_min, t_max = 0.0, 1.0
# Number of sample points
N_collocation = 10000   # interior collocation points for PDE residual
N_dirichlet = 1000      # points on Dirichlet portion of boundary
N_neumann = 1000        # points on Neumann portion
N_initial = 1000        # points for initial conditions

key = jax.random.PRNGKey(0)

# Sample collocation points in the interior of the space-time domain (Monte Carlo sampling)
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
X_coll = jax.random.uniform(
    subkey, shape=(N_collocation, 4),
    minval=jnp.array([x_min, y_min, z_min, t_min]),
    maxval=jnp.array([x_max, y_max, z_max, t_max])
)

# Sample Dirichlet boundary points (space + time on Gamma_D).
# For example, let's assume x=0 face is Dirichlet (for all y,z) and all times.
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
y_bd = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_dirichlet,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
z_bd = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_dirichlet,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
t_bd = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_dirichlet,), minval=t_min, maxval=t_max)
# Assemble points on the x=0 plane (Dirichlet boundary):
X_dirichlet = jnp.stack([jnp.full((N_dirichlet,), x_min), y_bd, z_bd, t_bd], axis=1)

# Sample Neumann boundary points (e.g., x = x_max face as Gamma_N).
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
y_nb = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_neumann,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
z_nb = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_neumann,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
t_nb = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_neumann,), minval=t_min, maxval=t_max)
X_neumann = jnp.stack([jnp.full((N_neumann,), x_max), y_nb, z_nb, t_nb], axis=1)
# Normals for Neumann points (for x=x_max face, outward normal is +x direction):
normals_neumann = jnp.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])  # normal vector on x_max face
normals_neumann = jnp.tile(normals_neumann, (N_neumann, 1))  # replicate for all points

# Sample initial condition points at t = 0 (for initial u and v).
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
x_ini = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_initial,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
y_ini = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_initial,))
key, subkey = jax.random.split(key)
z_ini = jax.random.uniform(subkey, shape=(N_initial,))
# time is zero for all initial points:
t_ini = jnp.zeros((N_initial,))
X_init = jnp.stack([x_ini, y_ini, z_ini, t_ini], axis=1)

# Define the boundary condition functions and initial condition functions:
def dirichlet_bc_value(x, y, z, t):
    """Dirichlet boundary condition u = g_D on Gamma_D (here assumed u=0 for simplicity)."""
    return 0.0  # for example, fixed zero Dirichlet BC

def neumann_bc_value(x, y, z, t):
    """Neumann boundary condition value on Gamma_N for ∂u/∂n (flux)."""
    return 0.0  # e.g., zero-flux (Neumann) boundary

def initial_u(x, y, z):
    """Initial displacement u(x, y, z, 0)."""
    # Example: a small Gaussian pulse as initial condition (can be adjusted as needed).
    return jnp.exp(-100 * ((x-0.5)**2 + (y-0.5)**2 + (z-0.5)**2))

def initial_v(x, y, z):
    """Initial velocity ∂u/∂t (x, y, z, 0)."""
    return 0.0  # assume initial velocity is zero for simplicity

# Physics-informed loss calculation.
# We use vmap (vectorization) to apply model and compute residuals for batches of points efficiently.

# Create a convenient function to predict u given inputs (to avoid repeatedly writing model.apply).
def u_pred(params, x):
    """Predict u(x,t) using the neural network with given parameters."""
    return model.apply(params, rng, x)  # Haiku model apply (rng not used since no randomness in model)

# Compute the PDE residual at a single collocation point.
def pde_residual_point(params, x):
    """
    Compute the physics residual R = ∂_{tt}u - c(x)^2 ∇^2 u at a single point (x,y,z,t).
    """
    # We will use automatic differentiation to get second derivatives.
    # Define a scalar function for u at fixed params to differentiate easily:
    def u_scalar(coord):
        # coord is a length-4 array [x,y,z,t]
        return u_pred(params, coord).reshape(())  # scalar output
    # Compute second time derivative:
    # First time derivative:
    dudt = jax.grad(u_scalar)(x)[3]          # index 3 corresponds to t component
    d2udt2 = jax.grad(lambda X: jax.grad(u_scalar)(X)[3])(x)[3]  # derivative of dudt w.rt t
    # Compute Laplacian (∇^2 u = ∂^2u/∂x^2 + ∂^2u/∂y^2 + ∂^2u/∂z^2):
    # We can get the Hessian of u_scalar and sum spatial diagonal entries:
    H = jax.hessian(u_scalar)(x)            # Hessian matrix (4x4: x,y,z,t)
    laplace_u = H[0,0] + H[1,1] + H[2,2]    # sum of second derivatives in x,y,z
    # Evaluate wave speed at this spatial point:
    c_val = c_function(x[:3])
    # Residual of wave PDE:
    return d2udt2 - (c_val**2) * laplace_u

# Vectorize the residual computation for all collocation points:
pde_residuals = jax.vmap(lambda X: pde_residual_point(params, X))(X_coll)
# Compute the volume (domain) loss as the mean squared residual (MC approximation to weak form integral):
vol_loss = jnp.mean(pde_residuals**2)  # ≈ ∫(∂_{tt}u - c^2∇^2 u)^2 / |Ω|, drives residual -> 0

# Dirichlet boundary loss (MSE on Gamma_D):
u_pred_dir = jax.vmap(lambda X: u_pred(params, X).reshape(()))(X_dirichlet)      # network predictions on Dirichlet boundary
u_true_dir = jax.vmap(lambda X: dirichlet_bc_value(X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3]))(X_dirichlet)
bc_dir_loss = jnp.mean((u_pred_dir - u_true_dir)**2)  # enforce u ≈ g_D

# Neumann boundary loss (MSE on Gamma_N for normal derivative):
# Compute predicted normal derivative ∂u/∂n via grad:
normal_deriv_pred = jax.vmap(
    lambda X: jnp.dot(jax.grad(lambda coord: u_pred(params, coord).reshape(()))(X)[:3], jnp.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]))
)(X_neumann)
# ^ Here jax.grad gives ∇u = (∂x u, ∂y u, ∂z u, ∂t u); we take [:3] for spatial gradient and dot with normal = (1,0,0).
normal_deriv_true = jax.vmap(lambda X: neumann_bc_value(X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3]))(X_neumann)
bc_neu_loss = jnp.mean((normal_deriv_pred - normal_deriv_true)**2)

# Initial condition losses at t = 0:
# Loss for u(x,0) = u0(x):
u_pred_init = jax.vmap(lambda X: u_pred(params, X).reshape(()))(X_init)
u_true_init = jax.vmap(lambda X: initial_u(X[0], X[1], X[2]))(X_init)
init_u_loss = jnp.mean((u_pred_init - u_true_init)**2)
# Loss for ∂t u(x,0) = v0(x):
u_t_pred_init = jax.vmap(lambda X: jax.grad(lambda coord: u_pred(params, coord).reshape(()))(X)[3])(X_init)
u_t_true_init = jax.vmap(lambda X: initial_v(X[0], X[1], X[2]))(X_init)
init_v_loss = jnp.mean((u_t_pred_init - u_t_true_init)**2)

# Total loss is sum of all components:
total_loss = vol_loss + bc_dir_loss + bc_neu_loss + init_u_loss + init_v_loss

print("Initial total loss:", float(total_loss))
# (At initialization, the loss will likely be large since the network is untrained.)
```

The above constructs the full PINN loss. Next, we can **differentiate this loss w\.r.t. network parameters** to train the model:

```python
# Define a loss function for optimization (with params as argument):
def loss_fn(params):
    # This function recomputes the total loss given parameters.
    # (In practice, we'd use closures or partials to avoid rewriting, but for clarity we inline computations.)
    # Physics residual (weak form):
    pde_res = jax.vmap(lambda X: pde_residual_point(params, X))(X_coll)
    vol_loss = jnp.mean(pde_res**2)
    # Dirichlet BC loss:
    u_dir = jax.vmap(lambda X: u_pred(params, X).reshape(()))(X_dirichlet)
    g_dir = jax.vmap(lambda X: dirichlet_bc_value(X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3]))(X_dirichlet)
    bc_dir = jnp.mean((u_dir - g_dir)**2)
    # Neumann BC loss:
    normal_pred = jax.vmap(lambda X: jnp.dot(jax.grad(lambda coord: u_pred(params, coord).reshape(()))(X)[:3],
                                            jnp.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])))(X_neumann)
    normal_true = jax.vmap(lambda X: neumann_bc_value(X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3]))(X_neumann)
    bc_neu = jnp.mean((normal_pred - normal_true)**2)
    # Initial conditions loss:
    u0_pred = jax.vmap(lambda X: u_pred(params, X).reshape(()))(X_init)
    u0_true = jax.vmap(lambda X: initial_u(X[0], X[1], X[2]))(X_init)
    init_u = jnp.mean((u0_pred - u0_true)**2)
    ut0_pred = jax.vmap(lambda X: jax.grad(lambda coord: u_pred(params, coord).reshape(()))(X)[3])(X_init)
    ut0_true = jax.vmap(lambda X: initial_v(X[0], X[1], X[2]))(X_init)
    init_v = jnp.mean((ut0_pred - ut0_true)**2)
    return vol_loss + bc_dir + bc_neu + init_u + init_v

# Use JAX's automatic differentiation to get gradients of the loss w.rt parameters:
grad_fn = jax.value_and_grad(loss_fn)

# Choose an optimizer (Optax) for training, e.g., Adam or L-BFGS.
optimizer = optax.adam(learning_rate=1e-3)
opt_state = optimizer.init(params)

# Training loop (gradient descent):
for step in range(10001):
    loss_val, grads = grad_fn(params)        # compute loss and gradients
    updates, opt_state = optimizer.update(grads, opt_state, params)
    params = optax.apply_updates(params, updates)
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"Step {step}: loss = {float(loss_val):.6f}")
```

This loop will adjust `params` to minimize the total loss. We use `jax.value_and_grad` to compute loss and gradient in one pass for efficiency. The `adam` optimizer (from Optax) is used here for illustration, but one could also use more advanced optimizers or even full-batch L-BFGS for faster convergence on PINN problems【15†L251-L259】.

**Key points in the code:**

- We leveraged **`jax.grad` and `jax.hessian`** to compute the necessary derivatives (time second derivative and spatial Laplacian) for the PDE residual. This is done inside `pde_residual_point`. Automatic differentiation provides these derivatives to machine precision【15†L235-L243】, avoiding manual finite differences.
- We used **`jax.vmap`** to efficiently apply our computations to a batch of points. For example, `jax.vmap(pde_residual_point)` is applied over all collocation points to get a vector of residuals. Similarly, we vmap the model predictions and boundary condition functions over batches of points for the loss terms.
- The **Dirichlet BC loss** `bc_dir_loss` is computed by evaluating the network at sampled boundary points and comparing to the known boundary value (`dirichlet_bc_value`). This is a soft enforcement approach as discussed.
- The **Neumann BC loss** `bc_neu_loss` uses `jax.grad(u_pred)` to get $\nabla u$ at each Neumann point, takes the dot product with the normal vector, and compares to the target flux. This implements the Neumann condition in the loss.
- The **initial conditions losses** (`init_u_loss`, `init_v_loss`) enforce $u(x,0)=u\_0(x)$ and $\partial\_t u(x,0)=v\_0(x)$ by evaluating the network at $t=0$ and using `jax.grad` to get the time derivative.
- We approximate the **weak-form residual integral** by a Monte Carlo average (`jnp.mean` of residual$^2$ over random points). In expectation, this equals the $L^2$ norm of the residual over the domain (up to domain volume scaling). By minimizing this, we drive the weighted residual to zero【9†L249-L257】.
- The code is structured in a modular way (with clear functions for $u\_{\theta}$ prediction and residual computation) to allow easy extension. For instance, one could incorporate more complex architectures (e.g. adding **positional encoding** for multi-resolution features to the input before the MLP, or using different activation functions to address spectral bias【6†L65-L72】) without changing the loss formulation.
- The use of JAX/Haiku means the code is fully differentiable and can be JIT-compiled for efficiency. In practice, we could add `@jax.jit` to `loss_fn` or even to the training step for significant speed-ups after the initial compilation.

This template can be adapted to real 3D heterogeneous data by modifying `c_function` to match the spatially-varying wave speed profile (e.g., interpolating from a given dataset or formula) and adjusting the domain/boundary sampling accordingly. The approach naturally extends to more complex boundary shapes (one would sample surface points and normals for those geometries). By minimizing the above loss, the PINN will learn an implicit function $u\_{\theta}(x,y,z,t)$ that satisfies the 3D wave equation in heterogeneous media, along with the specified mixed boundary conditions and initial conditions.

## Step 3: Physics Formalism and Experiment Stack

- **Governing PDE (Strong Form):** The 2D heterogeneous acoustic wave equation is\
  $$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2}(x,y,t) - \nabla!\cdot!\big(c^2(x,y)\nabla u(x,y,t)\big) = 0,$$\
  where $u(x,y,t)$ is the pressure field, and $c(x,y)$ is the spatially varying wave speed. If density $\rho(x,y)$ also varies, a general form is $\rho(x,y)u\_{tt} - \nabla\cdot(\mu(x,y)\nabla u) = 0$. For simplicity, we assume constant density and focus on $c(x,y)$.

- **Weak-Form Residual:** Multiplying by a test function $\phi(x,y)$ and integrating over domain $\Omega$ gives the weak form:\
  $$R(u,\phi) ;=;\int\_\Omega \phi,u\_{tt},d\Omega + \int\_\Omega c^2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla u,d\Omega - \int\_{\partial\Omega} \phi,(c^2\nabla u\cdot \mathbf{n}),ds = 0.$$\
  We integrate by parts the spatial divergence term, yielding a boundary term involving normal derivative. In practice, the PINN enforces the interior PDE residual and boundary conditions separately.

- **Boundary Conditions in Loss:**
  - *Dirichlet BC:* If $u$ is specified (e.g. $u=0$ or a known source function) on boundary $\Gamma\_D$, we add a term $\mathrm{MSE}(u\_{\text{NN}}, u\_{\text{true}})$ on $\Gamma\_D$ to the loss. This penalizes deviations from the prescribed value.
  - *Absorbing (Sommerfeld) BC:* For an open domain, we enforce an absorbing boundary condition $\partial u/\partial t + c,\partial u/\partial n = 0$ on $\Gamma\_{\text{abs}}$. In the loss function we include $\mathrm{MSE}(u\_t + c,u\_n, 0)$ for points on $\Gamma\_{\text{abs}}$. This term softly imposes the radiation condition by treating $u\_t + c,u\_n$ as an additional residual to minimize. Proper weighting of this term (via a hyperparameter) controls how strictly the absorbing BC is enforced.

- **PINN Code Template (PyTorch):** Below is a simplified PyTorch skeleton for a 2D wave PINN. It defines a neural network for $u(x,y,t)$, computes the PDE residual via autodifferentiation, and includes loss terms for PDE, Dirichlet BC, and absorbing BC. (*In practice, adjust layers/activation and domain-specific details as needed.*)

  ```python

  import torch.nn as nn

  # Neural network for u(x,y,t)
  class PINN(nn.Module):
      def __init__(self, layers):
          super(PINN, self).__init__()
          self.net = nn.Sequential()
          for i in range(len(layers)-1):
              self.net.add_module(f"layer{i}", nn.Linear(layers[i], layers[i+1]))
              if i < len(layers)-2:
                  self.net.add_module(f"act{i}", nn.Tanh())
      def forward(self, xy_t):
          # xy_t: tensor of shape [batch, 3] columns = (x, y, t)
          u = self.net(xy_t)
          return u

  # Instantiate PINN
  layers = [3, 64, 64, 64, 1]  # input 3 dims (x,y,t) to 1 output
  model = PINN(layers)

  # Physics-informed loss computation
  def pinn_loss(model, xy_t_colloc, xy_t_dirichlet, xy_t_absorb, u_D_value):
      # Compute predictions
      u_colloc = model(xy_t_colloc)          # inside domain
      u_D_pred   = model(xy_t_dirichlet)     # Dirichlet boundary points
      u_abs_pred = model(xy_t_absorb)        # Absorbing boundary points

      # Compute PDE residual for collocation points
      # Use autograd: compute second time derivative and spatial Laplacian
      grads = torch.autograd.grad(u_colloc, xy_t_colloc, 
                  grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_colloc), create_graph=True)[0]
      u_x = grads[:,0:1]    # ∂u/∂x
      u_y = grads[:,1:2]    # ∂u/∂y
      u_t = grads[:,2:3]    # ∂u/∂t
      # second derivatives
      u_xx = torch.autograd.grad(u_x, xy_t_colloc, 
                  grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_x), create_graph=True)[0][:,0:1]
      u_yy = torch.autograd.grad(u_y, xy_t_colloc, 
                  grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_y), create_graph=True)[0][:,1:2]
      u_tt = torch.autograd.grad(u_t, xy_t_colloc, 
                  grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_t), create_graph=True)[0][:,2:3]

      # PDE residual r = u_tt - c^2*(u_xx + u_yy)
      c = wave_speed(xy_t_colloc[:,0:1], xy_t_colloc[:,1:2])  # function for c(x,y)
      pde_res = u_tt - (c**2)*(u_xx + u_yy)

      # Loss terms
      loss_pde = torch.mean(pde_res**2)  # interior PDE residual loss
      loss_dir = torch.mean((u_D_pred - u_D_value)**2)  # Dirichlet MSE
      # Absorbing BC: approximate ∂u/∂n ≈ u_x*n_x + u_y*n_y; assume normal = outward in code context
      # For simplicity assume n points outward on boundary (precomputed).
      # Here we demonstrate form: (u_t + c * (n·∇u))
      normal = get_normals(xy_t_absorb)   # user-defined normals at absorb BC
      u_abs_t = torch.autograd.grad(u_abs_pred, xy_t_absorb, 
                   grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_abs_pred), create_graph=True)[0][:,2:3]
      u_abs_x = torch.autograd.grad(u_abs_pred, xy_t_absorb, 
                   grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_abs_pred), create_graph=True)[0][:,0:1]
      u_abs_y = torch.autograd.grad(u_abs_pred, xy_t_absorb, 
                   grad_outputs=torch.ones_like(u_abs_pred), create_graph=True)[0][:,1:2]
      c_abs = wave_speed(xy_t_absorb[:,0:1], xy_t_absorb[:,1:2])
      u_n = u_abs_x*normal[:,0:1] + u_abs_y*normal[:,1:2]
      absorb_res = u_abs_t + c_abs * u_n
      loss_abs = torch.mean(absorb_res**2)

      # Weighted sum of losses
      loss = loss_pde + 10.0*loss_dir + 5.0*loss_abs  # weights are hyperparameters
      return loss, loss_pde, loss_dir, loss_abs
  ```

Key formatting points: single-column preprint style with numbered sections, 12pt font, and figures/tables with captions. Ensure references follow the numbered style (e.g. \[1], \[2]). Equations should be numbered sequentially. Remove any draft or review-specific commands. A quick review of the JCP guide suggests a 25-page limit for full submission.

- **Abstract (≤200 words):** We rewrite the abstract concisely. For example:\
  *Abstract:* *We propose “WavePINN-NIF-ComplexMedia”, a physics-informed neural network framework for 2D acoustic waves in heterogeneous media. The method couples a traditional PINN solver with a novel Neural Implicit Field (NIF) network that encodes spatial variations of wave speed. This enables efficient learning of sharp interfaces and high-frequency components. Dirichlet and absorbing (Sommerfeld) boundary conditions are enforced via tailored loss terms. In numerical experiments on complex layered and random-media models, WavePINN-NIF significantly outperforms a standard PINN in accuracy (order-of-magnitude error reduction) while using fewer training points. The approach is implemented in PyTorch and demonstrates scalability on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Our contributions include the NIF-enhanced PINN architecture, weak-form PDE residual derivation, and an open-source reproducible workflow. This work advances PINNs for realistic wave propagation problems, bridging computational physics and machine learning.* (Word count \~ 150.)\
  This abstract highlights the novelty (NIF network), main results (accuracy improvement), and relevance to computational physics.

- **Cover Letter:** Draft a succinct cover letter addressed to the *Journal of Computational Physics* editor. Emphasize:
  1. **Novelty:** “This work introduces a new PINN architecture (WavePINN-NIF) that integrates an implicit neural representation of complex media into the wave equation solver.”
  2. **Technical depth:** “We derive the weak-form PINN loss for heterogeneous media, implement it in PyTorch, and validate on challenging benchmarks.”
  3. **Results:** “Results show significant error reduction vs. baseline PINNs in 2D heterogeneous domains.”
  4. **Relevance:** “This contributes to computational physics by enabling physics-informed ML methods to handle realistic acoustics problems relevant to JCP readership.”
  5. **Compliance:** mention no conflict of interest, readiness to suggest reviewers, etc.\
     Keep it to one page (\~200-300 words), formal tone, and include manuscript title and authors.

We acknowledge the environmental footprint of large GPU computations and commit to reporting such usage transparently.”

“**Verification Steps:** To ensure scientific validity, we will cross-check results by (a) comparing PINN solutions against analytical or high-resolution numerical solutions in test cases, (b) performing grid-convergence studies (refining collocation sampling until solutions converge), (c) conducting ablation tests (e.g. disabling the NIF component), and (d) sharing code and data so that independent replication is possible. We recommend peer reviewers verify numerical results by running our open-source code under documented conditions.”

These measures align with best practices for reproducibility and ethical transparency.
