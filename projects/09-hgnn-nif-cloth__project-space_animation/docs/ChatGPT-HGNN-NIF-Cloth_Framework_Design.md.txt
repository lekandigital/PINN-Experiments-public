### Summary of Trends, Limitations, and Gaps

Over the past several years, **graph neural networks (GNNs)** have increasingly incorporated **multi-resolution representations** to improve learning on complex structures. Early works like Graph U-Net (2019) and SAGPool (2019) introduced *hierarchical graph pooling* mechanisms that enabled GNNs to capture global context and multi-scale information by coarsening and refining graphs【31†L57-L65】【32†L55-L63】. This trend addressed the “flat” nature of classic GNNs, mitigating issues like oversmoothing and limited receptive fields. Subsequent research (e.g. LGRPool 2025) further emphasized aligning local and global features in pooled graphs to preserve important topology across levels. **Dynamic graph pooling** – selecting nodes based on data-dependent criteria (attention scores, learned projections) – became a key theme, allowing adaptable graph resolution for each input graph.

In parallel, GNN-based **learned physics simulators** emerged as a powerful approach to model dynamics of complex systems. Sanchez-Gonzalez *et al.* (2020) showed that message-passing GNNs can learn to simulate fluids and deformable materials with impressive generalization【33†L51-L59】. However, cloth simulation remained challenging due to the extreme deformations and contact-rich interactions (e.g. self-collisions) of cloth. Pfaff *et al.* (2021) tackled this by introducing **MeshGraphNets**, using meshes (graph-structured grids) and even allowing the network to adapt mesh resolution during forward simulation【2†L57-L64】. This hierarchy of meshes improved efficiency and enabled **resolution-invariant** learning of cloth dynamics, but the method still had limitations: for instance, the original MeshGraphNets struggled with very fast motions and rotational movements of cloth, as noted by later studies【5†L159-L167】.

Recent work on **neural cloth simulation** has focused on overcoming these limitations. One direction has been adding explicit physics knowledge into GNN models: e.g. MeshGraphNetRP (2023) included physical priors (stretching and bending energies) and recurrent memory to handle oscillatory cloth behavior【5†L111-L119】, making learned simulators more stable on long, dynamic sequences. Another direction is using *unsupervised or self-supervised* learning to train cloth models without requiring expensive ground-truth simulations. Bertiche *et al.* (2022) achieved this by embedding a physics optimizer within the training loop【26†L37-L45】, allowing their network to learn realistic cloth draping and wrinkles from scratch. They also identified the importance of separating static and dynamic components of cloth motion in the model’s latent space, to improve generalization【26†L41-L49】. Despite these advances, learned GNN simulators still face challenges in capturing fine-grained geometric detail (e.g. tiny wrinkles) and handling topological changes or frequent collisions.

Enter the emerging field of **neural implicit fields (NIFs)** for cloth. Neural implicit representations (such as signed distance fields parameterized by neural networks) can represent surfaces with arbitrary detail and continuous resolution. Sitzmann *et al.* (2020) showed that sinusoidal representation networks (SIRENs) excel at modeling high-frequency details and even solving PDEs like Eikonal equations for SDFs【36†L31-L39】. In 2024, Kairanda *et al.* introduced NeuralClothSim, the first approach to integrate cloth physics simulation with an implicit neural surface【19†L33-L41】. By encoding the cloth’s deformation in the weights of a network (a *neural deformation field*), their method sidesteps the need for a fixed mesh resolution and can query cloth shape at any point in space【19†L37-L45】. This implicit approach naturally handles fine details (since resolution is not fixed) and offers a differentiable formulation of cloth dynamics. However, a pure implicit method may lack the explicit discrete structure needed to enforce certain physical constraints or easily incorporate known structure (e.g. yarn or mesh connectivity).

**Research Gap & Opportunity:** A hybrid approach that combines **hierarchical GNNs** and **neural implicit fields** promises to leverage the strengths of both. GNN-based simulators provide structured, physically grounded predictions (e.g. preserving network neighborhood relationships corresponding to cloth yarn connectivity and enabling message-passing of forces), while implicit decoders can capture the high-resolution surface geometry without enumerating it in the state. So far, these two paradigms have mostly been explored separately. Bridging them is non-trivial – it requires the GNN to produce a suitable latent representation of the cloth state that an implicit decoder can use to reconstruct geometry. It also requires handling two different representations (graph and continuous field) in training. No known prior work fully integrates a multi-resolution **graph simulator with an implicit surface decoder** for cloth, making this an open research area. The **HGNN-NIF-Cloth** project aims to fill this gap by using a *Hierarchical Graph Neural Network* to simulate cloth physics at multiple scales, coupled with a *Neural Implicit Field* decoder (SIREN) that produces a detailed cloth surface. This hybrid could achieve real-time performance (by operating on a coarse graph when appropriate) while maintaining accuracy (by decoding fine details implicitly). Key research challenges remain, such as how to **adaptively trade off** between coarse and fine resolutions during simulation and how to ensure consistency between the graph-based state and the implicit field. The following steps outline the development of this integrated system.

## Step 2: Data Generation – Cloth Simulation and Graph/SDF Extraction

**Objective:** Generate a training dataset of cloth simulations with corresponding multi-level graph structures and signed distance field (SDF) volumes. We use Blender’s physics engine to simulate cloth, then for each frame, we extract the cloth mesh at multiple resolutions (fine and coarse) and compute an SDF volume representing the cloth’s surface. Finally, we store the data in a convenient format (HDF5) for training.

Below is a **Python script** (to be run inside Blender) that accomplishes this. It sets up a cloth simulation, runs it frame by frame, and captures per-frame node positions (for fine and decimated meshes), adjacency lists, and SDF voxel grids. Inline comments explain each step, and necessary dependencies (`bpy` for Blender, `bmesh` for mesh editing, `numpy`, `h5py`) are noted. This script assumes Blender’s Python environment has access to `numpy` and `h5py` (you may need to install `h5py` in Blender’s Python). After running the simulation, the output is saved to an HDF5 file (e.g., `cloth_data.h5`) containing datasets for node coordinates, edges, and SDF values per frame.

```python
# generate_cloth_data.py – Run in Blender to simulate cloth and extract training data

import bpy
import bmesh
import numpy as np
import h5py
import math
import sys

# Parse output file path from arguments (Blender passes args after '--')
output_path = "cloth_data.h5"
if "--" in sys.argv:
    argv = sys.argv[sys.argv.index("--")+1:]
    if len(argv) > 0:
        output_path = argv[0]
print(f"Output data file: {output_path}")

# ----------------------
# 1. Create cloth object
# ----------------------
# Add a planar mesh (e.g., a subdivided plane) to act as the cloth
bpy.ops.mesh.primitive_plane_add(size=1.0, location=(0, 0, 0))
cloth_obj = bpy.context.object
cloth_obj.name = "Cloth"
# Subdivide the plane to increase mesh resolution (more vertices = finer cloth mesh)
subdiv_level = 20  # number of cuts (controls fine mesh resolution)
bpy.ops.object.mode_set(mode='EDIT')
bm = bmesh.from_edit_mesh(cloth_obj.data)
bmesh.ops.subdivide_edges(bm, edges=bm.edges, cuts=subdiv_level, use_grid_fill=True)
bmesh.update_edit_mesh(cloth_obj.data)
bpy.ops.object.mode_set(mode='OBJECT')
print(f"Created cloth mesh with {len(cloth_obj.data.vertices)} vertices.")

# Assign a cloth physics modifier to the mesh
cloth_mod = cloth_obj.modifiers.new(name="ClothSim", type='CLOTH')
cloth_settings = cloth_mod.settings
cloth_settings.quality = 5        # simulation quality (trade-off speed/accuracy)
cloth_settings.mass = 0.3         # mass of cloth vertices
cloth_settings.gravity = (0.0, 0.0, -9.81)  # gravity in Blender's Z direction
# Pin the top edge vertices so cloth is attached at top (to simulate a hanging cloth)
# Create a vertex group for pinning
vg = cloth_obj.vertex_groups.new(name="PinGroup")
# Find top edge vertices (e.g., those with max y or z depending on orientation)
top_verts = [v.index for v in cloth_obj.data.vertices if v.co[1] > 0.49]  # assuming plane lies in X-Y
vg.add(top_verts, 1.0, 'ADD')
cloth_settings.pin_stiffness = 1.0
cloth_settings.vertex_group_mass = vg.name  # use vertex group for pin (mass=0 means fixed)
# (Alternatively, could pin corners or apply constraints as needed)

# (Optional) Add wind force to make cloth move dynamically
wind = bpy.data.objects.new("Wind", None)
wind.field.type = 'WIND'
wind.field.strength = 5.0
bpy.context.collection.objects.link(wind)
wind.location = (0, 0, 0)        # position wind at origin
wind.field.flow = 1.0            # flow of wind
# Orient wind to blow along -X (for example)
wind.rotation_euler = (0, math.radians(90), 0)

# ------------------------
# 2. Run the cloth simulation
# ------------------------
scene = bpy.context.scene
scene.frame_start = 1
scene.frame_end = 100    # simulate 100 frames (adjust as needed)
scene.frame_set(scene.frame_start)
# Ensure the simulation cache is clear before starting
if cloth_obj.modifiers["ClothSim"].point_cache:
    cloth_obj.modifiers["ClothSim"].point_cache.clear()

# Prepare data storage
num_frames = scene.frame_end - scene.frame_start + 1
# We will collect fine-level and coarse-level data for each frame
fine_positions = []
coarse_positions = []
fine_edge_indices = []
coarse_edge_indices = []
sdf_volumes = []

# Define a helper to compute SDF volume for a given set of vertices and faces
def compute_sdf(vertices, faces, grid_res=32, padding=0.1):
    # vertices: (N,3) array of vertex coords; faces: list of face index triplets
    verts = np.array(vertices)
    # Determine bounding box of the cloth
    min_bound = verts.min(axis=0) - padding
    max_bound = verts.max(axis=0) + padding
    grid = np.linspace  # alias for brevity
    xs = grid(min_bound[0], max_bound[0], grid_res)
    ys = grid(min_bound[1], max_bound[1], grid_res)
    zs = grid(min_bound[2], max_bound[2], grid_res)
    sdf = np.zeros((grid_res, grid_res, grid_res), dtype=np.float32)
    # For each grid cell center, compute distance to cloth surface:
    # Here we approximate by distance to nearest vertex (faster but coarse).
    # In practice, one would compute distance to the nearest point on any triangle.
    for ix, x in enumerate(xs):
        for iy, y in enumerate(ys):
            for iz, z in enumerate(zs):
                pt = np.array([x, y, z])
                # Compute distance to nearest vertex (proxy for surface distance)
                dist = np.min(np.linalg.norm(verts - pt, axis=1))
                # Determine sign of distance: we can use a simple heuristic by checking on which side of cloth normal the point is.
                # For simplicity, we set negative sign if point is below the cloth (assuming cloth oriented initially horizontal).
                # A robust approach would raycast to determine if point is inside an enclosed volume.
                sign = 1.0
                # If cloth is roughly horizontal, use z coordinate relative to cloth mean plane as sign:
                if pt[2] < np.mean(verts[:, 2]):
                    sign = -1.0
                sdf[ix, iy, iz] = sign * dist
    return sdf

# Simulation loop: step through frames and record data
for frame in range(scene.frame_start, scene.frame_end + 1):
    scene.frame_set(frame)  # advance simulation by one frame
    # Access the evaluated mesh (with cloth deformations at this frame)
    depsgraph = bpy.context.evaluated_depsgraph_get()
    cloth_eval = cloth_obj.evaluated_get(depsgraph)
    mesh = cloth_eval.to_mesh()  # get mesh data at current frame
    # Get fine mesh vertex positions
    fine_coords = np.array([v.co[:] for v in mesh.vertices], dtype=np.float32)
    fine_positions.append(fine_coords)
    # Get fine mesh edges (list of vertex index pairs)
    fine_edges = [(e.vertices[0], e.vertices[1]) for e in mesh.edges]
    fine_edge_indices.append(np.array(fine_edges, dtype=np.int32))
    # Generate a coarse mesh representation (e.g., by decimating the fine mesh)
    # Here we use a simple strategy: take one vertex out of every group of 4 (this assumes structured mesh for simplicity).
    # More robust approach: use Blender's Decimate modifier or mesh clustering.
    coarse_idx = list(range(0, len(mesh.vertices), 4))
    if len(coarse_idx) < 4:
        coarse_idx = list(range(len(mesh.vertices)))  # ensure at least something
    coarse_coords = fine_coords[coarse_idx]
    coarse_positions.append(coarse_coords)
    # Build coarse edges by filtering fine edges to those connecting retained vertices
    coarse_edge_set = []
    retained = set(coarse_idx)
    for (i, j) in fine_edges:
        if i in retained and j in retained:
            coarse_edge_set.append((coarse_idx.index(i), coarse_idx.index(j)))
    coarse_edge_indices.append(np.array(coarse_edge_set, dtype=np.int32))
    # Compute SDF volume for the fine mesh
    faces = [tuple(p.vertices) for p in mesh.polygons]  # mesh faces (triangles/quads)
    sdf = compute_sdf(fine_coords, faces, grid_res=32)
    sdf_volumes.append(sdf)
    # Clean up the temporary mesh copy
    cloth_eval.to_mesh_clear()

# ------------------------
# 3. Save data to HDF5 file
# ------------------------
with h5py.File(output_path, 'w') as h5f:
    h5f.create_dataset('frames', data=np.arange(scene.frame_start, scene.frame_end+1), compression='gzip')
    h5f.create_dataset('fine_positions', data=np.array(fine_positions), compression='gzip')
    h5f.create_dataset('fine_edges', data=np.array(fine_edge_indices, dtype=object), **{"dtype": h5py.vlen_dtype('int32')})
    h5f.create_dataset('coarse_positions', data=np.array(coarse_positions), compression='gzip')
    h5f.create_dataset('coarse_edges', data=np.array(coarse_edge_indices, dtype=object), **{"dtype": h5py.vlen_dtype('int32')})
    h5f.create_dataset('sdf_volumes', data=np.array(sdf_volumes), compression='gzip')
print(f"Saved cloth simulation data for {num_frames} frames to {output_path}")
```

**Script explanation:** We programmatically create a cloth by adding a plane and subdividing it (to get a fine mesh of \~400 vertices, given 20 subdivisions each direction). We set up Blender’s cloth physics and optionally a wind force to induce motion. The top edge is pinned so the cloth hangs. We then simulate from frame 1 to 100, manually advancing the frame (which causes Blender to update the cloth simulation state). At each frame, we obtain the deformed mesh, record the **fine** vertex coordinates and edges, then derive a **coarse** graph by sampling one fourth of the vertices (this simplistic strategy assumes a structured mesh; more advanced methods could use a mesh decimation algorithm for irregular cloth). We also compute a 32³ grid **signed distance field (SDF)** around the cloth. For brevity, the SDF calculation here finds the nearest vertex distance and assigns a sign based on the point’s position relative to the cloth’s plane (in practice, one would compute distance to the mesh surface more exactly, e.g. using ray intersections to determine inside/outside). Finally, all data arrays are saved into an HDF5 file with datasets for easy loading in PyTorch (each dataset indexed by frame). The HDF5 contains: `fine_positions[frame_index, vertex_index, :]` (XYZ positions), `fine_edges[frame_index]` (list of edges as pairs of vertex indices), and similarly `coarse_positions`, `coarse_edges`, plus `sdf_volumes[frame_index, x, y, z]` for the SDF grid. This dataset will be used to train and validate our HGNN-NIF-Cloth model.

## Step 3: Model Implementation – Hierarchical GNN and SIREN Decoder

We now build the core model components in **PyTorch**. The architecture consists of two parts: (a) an **Adaptive Hierarchical Graph Neural Network (HGNN)** that processes the cloth simulation state at multiple resolutions, and (b) a **Neural Implicit Field decoder (SIREN)** that reconstructs the cloth’s continuous surface from the HGNN’s latent output.

**(a) Adaptive HGNN with energy-based resolution control:** The HGNN operates on a **fine graph** (detailed mesh of the cloth) and a **coarse graph** (simplified mesh). We assume the input data for the model includes both graphs (as obtained from Step 2). The HGNN uses a graph message-passing network on the fine level and the coarse level, and a mechanism to adaptively decide how much detail to use based on the cloth’s state. Specifically, we introduce an **“energy” metric** (e.g. total stretch energy) computed from the fine graph, and use it to control resolution: if the cloth deformation is mild (low energy), the model can rely more on the coarse representation for efficiency; if the cloth is under high stress (high energy, e.g. many wrinkles or fast motion), the model uses the fine resolution for accuracy. This adaptive logic is implemented as a gating in the forward pass. We also include a **cross-level attention** module (part (b) below) that allows information exchange between coarse and fine representations – the coarse graph can provide global context to the fine nodes, and fine graph details can refine the coarse prediction.

**(b) Cross-level attention mechanism:** We implement a custom cross-attention layer that takes **queries** from one graph’s node features and **keys/values** from another graph’s features. In our model, we’ll use it to let fine-level nodes attend to coarse-level nodes (or vice versa) to incorporate multi-scale information. This is similar to a transformer’s attention, but across two sets of nodes at different resolutions.

**(c) Latent-conditioned SIREN decoder:** We use a **Sinusoidal Representation Network (SIREN)**【36†L29-L38】 to decode the cloth surface as an implicit function. The SIREN is an MLP with sine activation functions, known to be effective for representing high-frequency details and signed distance fields (it can naturally represent complex geometry and even satisfy the differential constraints of SDFs like the Eikonal equation)【36†L31-L39】. Our SIREN will output an SDF value given a 3D point coordinate **and** conditioned on the cloth’s latent state. The conditioning is done by feeding a learned latent vector (produced by the HGNN) into the SIREN along with the point coordinate. In practice, this latent could be concatenated to the input of the SIREN or used to modulate its layers (here we choose concatenation for simplicity).

Below is the PyTorch code for these components. We ensure it’s fully commented and modular:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
import math

# (a) Graph Convolution Layer (simple implementation)
class GraphConv(nn.Module):
    """A simple graph convolution layer that aggregates neighbor features."""
    def __init__(self, in_feats, out_feats):
        super(GraphConv, self).__init__()
        self.W_self = nn.Linear(in_feats, out_feats, bias=False)
        self.W_nei = nn.Linear(in_feats, out_feats, bias=False)
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(out_feats))
    def forward(self, x, edges):
        """
        x: Tensor of shape (N, in_feats) for N nodes.
        edges: Tensor of shape (E, 2) containing pairs (u, v) for each edge.
        Returns: Tensor of shape (N, out_feats) after one message-passing step.
        """
        N = x.shape[0]
        # Linear transform of node features (self part)
        h_self = self.W_self(x)
        # Initialize neighbor aggregation sum
        h_nei = torch.zeros((N, h_self.shape[1]), device=x.device)
        # Aggregate messages from neighbors
        # We assume undirected edges; for each edge (u,v), add u's contribution to v and v's to u.
        for (u, v) in edges:
            # Add neighbor feature transformed by W_nei
            h_nei[v] += self.W_nei(x[u])
            h_nei[u] += self.W_nei(x[v])
        # Combine self and neighbor contributions, add bias
        h = h_self + h_nei + self.bias
        return h

# (b) Cross-Level Attention module
class CrossLevelAttention(nn.Module):
    """
    Cross-attention from one set of node features (query) to another (key/value).
    Allows fine-level nodes to attend to coarse-level information (or vice versa).
    """
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super(CrossLevelAttention, self).__init__()
        self.query_proj = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.key_proj   = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.value_proj = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.scale = math.sqrt(dim)
    def forward(self, feat_query, feat_key):
        """
        feat_query: Tensor (Nq, d) – features of query nodes (e.g., fine nodes).
        feat_key:   Tensor (Nk, d) – features of key/value nodes (e.g., coarse nodes).
        Returns: Tensor (Nq, d) – aggregated features for query nodes.
        """
        Q = self.query_proj(feat_query)    # shape (Nq, d)
        K = self.key_proj(feat_key)       # shape (Nk, d)
        V = self.value_proj(feat_key)     # shape (Nk, d)
        # Compute attention scores = Q * K^T (dot-product attention)
        # Expand dims for broadcasting: (Nq, 1, d) dot (1, Nk, d) -> (Nq, Nk)
        attn_scores = torch.matmul(Q, K.T) / self.scale
        attn_weights = F.softmax(attn_scores, dim=1)  # softmax over key nodes for each query
        # Weighted sum of value features
        out = torch.matmul(attn_weights, V)
        return out

# (a) Adaptive Hierarchical GNN
class AdaptiveHGNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_dim, hidden_dim):
        """
        Hierarchical GNN that processes fine and coarse graph levels with adaptive resolution.
        in_dim: feature size of each node's input (e.g., 3 if using position as features).
        hidden_dim: latent feature size for internal representations.
        """
        super(AdaptiveHGNN, self).__init__()
        # Graph conv layers for fine and coarse graphs
        self.fine_conv1 = GraphConv(in_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.fine_conv2 = GraphConv(hidden_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.coarse_conv1 = GraphConv(in_dim, hidden_dim)
        self.coarse_conv2 = GraphConv(hidden_dim, hidden_dim)
        # Cross-level attention to integrate coarse <-> fine features
        self.cross_attn = CrossLevelAttention(hidden_dim)
        # Learnable projection for global pooling (to get a single latent vector from coarse graph)
        self.global_pool = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim)
        # Threshold for energy-based resolution gating (can be tuned)
        self.energy_threshold = 0.1  
        # Store rest lengths for edges (for energy computation); to be set externally
        self.register_buffer('rest_lengths', None)
    def compute_energy(self, coords, edges):
        """Compute a simple spring energy = sum of squared stretch for all edges."""
        if self.rest_lengths is None:
            return 0.0
        energy = 0.0
        for e_idx, (u, v) in enumerate(edges):
            # current edge length:
            dist = torch.norm(coords[u] - coords[v])
            # rest length for this edge (assuming rest_lengths set as [len(edge0), len(edge1), ...])
            L0 = self.rest_lengths[e_idx] if e_idx < len(self.rest_lengths) else dist
            stretch = (dist - L0) / (L0 + 1e-6)
            energy += stretch**2
        return energy.item()
    def forward(self, fine_x, fine_edges, coarse_x, coarse_edges):
        """
        fine_x: (Nf, in_dim) features for fine graph nodes.
        fine_edges: (Ef, 2) edges for fine graph.
        coarse_x: (Nc, in_dim) features for coarse graph nodes.
        coarse_edges: (Ec, 2) edges for coarse graph.
        Returns:
          fine_out: updated fine node features,
          coarse_out: updated coarse node features,
          latent: global latent vector representing cloth state.
        """
        # Step 1: Message passing on fine and coarse graphs (first layer)
        fine_h1 = F.relu(self.fine_conv1(fine_x, fine_edges))
        coarse_h1 = F.relu(self.coarse_conv1(coarse_x, coarse_edges))
        # Step 2: Cross-level information exchange.
        # Let fine nodes attend to coarse nodes to get global context
        fine_from_coarse = self.cross_attn(fine_h1, coarse_h1)
        # (We could also do coarse_from_fine = self.cross_attn(coarse_h1, fine_h1) if needed.)
        # Fuse the attended coarse info into fine features (e.g., by addition)
        fine_h1 = fine_h1 + fine_from_coarse
        # Step 3: Second layer of message passing (refine features)
        fine_h2 = F.relu(self.fine_conv2(fine_h1, fine_edges))
        coarse_h2 = F.relu(self.coarse_conv2(coarse_h1, coarse_edges))
        # Step 4: Adaptive resolution control based on energy
        # Compute current deformation energy on fine graph (using node positions if available as features)
        energy_val = self.compute_energy(fine_x, fine_edges) if fine_x.shape[1] >= 3 else 1.0
        if energy_val < self.energy_threshold:
            # Low energy: cloth is near-rest, use coarse output primarily
            # We skip fine detail refinement to save computation (in practice, we might have an if/else to not even compute fine_h2).
            fused_fine_out = fine_h1  # or a weighted blend favoring coarse (here we simply hold fine at first-layer result)
            fused_coarse_out = coarse_h2
        else:
            # High energy: incorporate fine detail
            fused_fine_out = fine_h2  # use full fine refinement
            fused_coarse_out = coarse_h2
        # Step 5: Global pooling to get a single latent vector from coarse graph (representing overall cloth state)
        # We do a simple average pooling followed by a linear layer
        coarse_mean = torch.mean(fused_coarse_out, dim=0, keepdim=True)  # shape (1, hidden_dim)
        latent = self.global_pool(coarse_mean)  # shape (1, hidden_dim)
        latent = torch.tanh(latent)  # bound latent to [-1,1] for stability
        return fused_fine_out, fused_coarse_out, latent.squeeze(0)
```

In `AdaptiveHGNN.forward`, we perform two rounds of graph convolution on both fine and coarse graphs. After the first round, we inject coarse-level information into fine-level features via `CrossLevelAttention`: each fine node attends to the coarse nodes’ features, producing a context vector that we add to the fine features (this helps the fine level be aware of global structure, improving long-range coherence). We could similarly allow coarse nodes to attend to fine nodes if needed (e.g. to refine coarse prediction with local details), but here we focus on fine receiving coarse context. Then we compute a second GNN layer on each graph.

The **energy-based gating** appears in Step 4: we calculate a simple spring energy from the fine graph’s current node coordinates (assuming the first 3 features of `fine_x` are spatial coordinates). If the normalized stretch energy is below a threshold, we assume the cloth is nearly static or flat, so the fine-grained updates might be unnecessary; thus we skip applying the second-layer fine update (or we could reduce its effect). In this toy implementation, we just carry over `fine_h1` if energy is low, meaning we effectively ignore the fine refinement (`fine_h2`). If energy is high (cloth is highly deformed or moving), we use the full fine detail (`fine_h2`). In practice, this gating could be smoother (e.g., a continuous weight rather than a hard if/else). The coarse branch `coarse_h2` is always used (since it’s cheap and provides global structure).

Finally, we perform a simple **global pooling** on the coarse graph to obtain a single **latent vector** representing the cloth’s state. Here we take the mean of the coarse node features and pass it through a linear layer (`global_pool`) to get `latent` (dimension = `hidden_dim`). This latent will condition the SIREN decoder. We choose coarse graph for pooling under the assumption that coarse nodes collectively summarize the cloth (since coarse nodes correspond to large patches of cloth). One could also concatenate pooled coarse and fine summaries, but using coarse alone keeps the latent compact and focused on low-frequency shape.

```python
# (c) SIREN: Sinusoidal Representation Network for implicit surface
class SirenLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_features, out_features, w0=1.0, c=6.0):
        super(SirenLayer, self).__init__()
        self.in_features = in_features
        self.linear = nn.Linear(in_features, out_features)
        self.w0 = w0
        # SIREN-specific weight initialization (to avoid trivial sine outputs):
        # For first layer, use c/in_features; for deeper layers use 1/in_features
        # Here we allow passing c for first layer scaling.
        with torch.no_grad():
            bound = (c / in_features) if w0 != 1.0 else (1.0 / in_features)
            self.linear.weight.uniform_(-bound, bound)
    def forward(self, x):
        # sin(w0 * W.x + b)
        return torch.sin(self.w0 * self.linear(x))

class SIRENDecoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, coord_dim=3, latent_dim=64, hidden_dim=128, hidden_layers=3):
        """
        SIREN network that maps (point coordinate, latent) -> SDF value.
        coord_dim: dimension of point coordinate (3 for 3D).
        latent_dim: dimension of conditioning latent vector.
        hidden_dim: number of units in hidden layers.
        hidden_layers: how many hidden layers (with sine activation).
        """
        super(SIRENDecoder, self).__init__()
        self.latent_dim = latent_dim
        # First layer: takes (coord + latent) as input
        self.layer1 = SirenLayer(coord_dim + latent_dim, hidden_dim, w0=30.0, c=6.0)  # higher w0 for first layer as per SIREN paper
        # Hidden layers
        self.hidden_layers = nn.ModuleList([
            SirenLayer(hidden_dim, hidden_dim, w0=1.0) for _ in range(hidden_layers - 1)
        ])
        # Final output layer (linear, no activation) to produce SDF value
        self.final_layer = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, 1)
        # Initialize final layer weights small (to output small initial SDF)
        with torch.no_grad():
            self.final_layer.weight.uniform_(-1e-4, 1e-4)
            self.final_layer.bias.uniform_(-1e-4, 1e-4)
    def forward(self, coords, latent):
        """
        coords: Tensor (M, 3) of 3D query points.
        latent: Tensor (latent_dim,) or (1, latent_dim) representing cloth state.
        Returns: Tensor (M,) of SDF values for each query point.
        """
        M = coords.shape[0]
        # If latent is 1D tensor, expand to batch of points
        if latent.dim() == 1:
            latent = latent.unsqueeze(0)
        latent = latent.expand(M, -1)  # shape (M, latent_dim)
        # Concatenate coordinate and latent to form input
        x = torch.cat([coords, latent], dim=1)  # (M, coord_dim + latent_dim)
        # Pass through SIREN layers
        x = self.layer1(x)
        for layer in self.hidden_layers:
            x = layer(x)
        sdf_vals = self.final_layer(x)
        return sdf_vals.squeeze(-1)  # (M,) output
```

The `SIRENDecoder` uses the approach from Sitzmann *et al.* (2020) for implicit neural representations【36†L29-L38】. We set `w0=30` in the first SirenLayer to allow the network to represent high-frequency details from the start, and use the recommended initialization constant `c=6` for the first layer to keep outputs in a reasonable range. Subsequent layers use `w0=1`. The input to the SIREN is a concatenation of a 3D point `(x,y,z)` and the `latent` vector from the HGNN (of size `latent_dim`, which we set to match the HGNN’s output dimension, e.g. 64). The network outputs a single scalar – the signed distance of that point to the cloth surface.

By conditioning on `latent`, the SIREN effectively represents a **deformable surface family**: different latent codes produce different SDF functions. During training, the HGNN will learn to produce latents such that the SIREN’s output matches the ground-truth cloth surface. In other words, the HGNN provides the **dynamic state** (deformation) and the SIREN provides a **continuous shape** decoder for that state.

Finally, we can wrap the HGNN and SIREN into one model for training convenience:

```python
# Combined HGNN + SIREN model
class HGNN_NIF_ClothModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, node_feat_dim=3, latent_dim=64, hidden_dim=64):
        super(HGNN_NIF_ClothModel, self).__init__()
        # Hierarchical GNN: input node features are 3D coords (and possibly velocity or other features)
        self.hgnn = AdaptiveHGNN(in_dim=node_feat_dim, hidden_dim=latent_dim)
        # SIREN decoder: maps coords+latent to SDF
        self.siren = SIRENDecoder(coord_dim=3, latent_dim=latent_dim, hidden_dim=128, hidden_layers=3)
    def forward(self, fine_graph, coarse_graph, query_points=None):
        """
        fine_graph: tuple (fine_node_feats, fine_edges)
        coarse_graph: tuple (coarse_node_feats, coarse_edges)
        query_points: (M,3) tensor of 3D points to query SDF (optional).
        Returns: dict with fine and coarse node outputs, latent, and optionally SDF values if query_points given.
        """
        fine_x, fine_edges = fine_graph  # fine_node_feats: (Nf,3), fine_edges: (Ef, 2)
        coarse_x, coarse_edges = coarse_graph
        # Run hierarchical GNN
        fine_out, coarse_out, latent = self.hgnn(fine_x, fine_edges, coarse_x, coarse_edges)
        output = {
            'fine_nodes': fine_out,
            'coarse_nodes': coarse_out,
            'latent': latent
        }
        # If query_points are provided, decode them to SDF using the SIREN
        if query_points is not None:
            sdf_vals = self.siren(query_points, latent)
            output['sdf'] = sdf_vals
        return output
```

This combined model accepts a fine and coarse graph (each with node features and edge indices). Typically, node features will include at least the 3D coordinates of the node (possibly augmented with velocity, previous step info, etc. – those can be concatenated as additional features). The `forward` method returns the updated node features for fine and coarse graphs, the latent vector, and if `query_points` are given, it also returns the predicted SDF values at those points. During training, we might sample a set of query points around the cloth and ask the model to predict their SDF, comparing to the ground truth SDF volume from the dataset.

**Technical rigor:** The HGNN uses simple linear message passing (summing neighbor contributions). More sophisticated GNN layers (GraphSAGE, GAT, etc.) could be substituted. The cross-level attention module provides a trainable way to fuse multi-resolution data. Our adaptive resolution mechanism is energy-based; other criteria (like error of a predictor, or learned gating via reinforcement learning) could be explored, but the current approach injects domain knowledge (physical energy) to inform the model when high resolution is needed.

With this implementation, we have a differentiable model from discrete graph inputs to a continuous implicit surface output. Next, we design appropriate **loss functions** and a training curriculum to train this model end-to-end.

## Step 4: Physics-Based Loss Functions and Curriculum Learning

To train the HGNN-NIF-Cloth model, we formulate a **multi-task loss** that supervises both the physics of the cloth (via graph-based losses) and the accuracy of the implicit surface (via SDF losses). Key losses include:

- **Edge Spring Loss:** This measures how well the model preserves the cloth’s internal constraints, such as spring lengths between connected nodes. We treat each edge of the cloth mesh as a spring with a rest length (taken from the initial undeformed cloth). The model (HGNN) predicts new node positions; we penalize deviations in edge lengths from their rest lengths or from ground-truth lengths. A typical formulation is:

$$
L\_{\text{spring}} ;=; \frac{1}{|E|}\sum\_{(i,j)\in E} \Big(| \mathbf{x}\_i - \mathbf{x}*j | - \ell*{ij}^{(0)}\Big)^2,,
$$

where $E$ is the set of mesh edges, $\mathbf{x}*i$ and $\mathbf{x}*j$ are the predicted positions of the nodes at the ends of edge $(i,j)$, and $\ell*{ij}^{(0)}$ is the rest length of that edge (computed from the cloth’s reference shape). This encourages the network to produce deformations that respect the cloth’s inextensibility (springs not too stretched/compressed). If training data provides ground-truth positions, one can instead use those: $\ell*{ij}^{(0)}$ could be replaced by the ground-truth distance at that timestep for a supervised loss. Our formulation encodes physical prior (spring energy) directly – a form of **physics loss** used in prior works【5†L149-L157】.

- **SDF Reconstruction Loss:** This supervises the implicit field output. After the model predicts an SDF via the SIREN (conditioned on the latent), we compare it to the ground-truth SDF of the cloth (from the simulation data). We sample a set of points in space (for example, all voxels in the 3D grid or a random subset of them) and compute an $L^2$ loss on SDF values:

$$
L\_{\text{SDF}} ;=; \frac{1}{N}\sum\_{k=1}^{N} \Big( \phi\_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}*k) - \phi*{\text{gt}}(\mathbf{p}\_k) \Big)^2,,
$$

where $\phi\_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}\_k)$ is the SDF value predicted by our model (SIREN) at point $\mathbf{p}*k$, and $\phi*{\text{gt}}(\mathbf{p}*k)$ is the ground-truth SDF value from the dataset for that point【19†L39-L47】. In practice, we sample points both near the cloth surface (to accurately capture the zero-level set) and further away (to ensure correct sign and distance in the field). We may also weight samples near the surface more heavily or include an **Eikonal regularization** term $||\nabla \phi*{\theta}(\mathbf{p})| - 1||^2$ to encourage the SDF to be valid (signed distance functions satisfy $||\nabla \phi|| = 1$ almost everywhere).

- (Optional) **Node Position Loss:** If we have ground-truth node positions from simulation, we can directly supervise the HGNN’s output by an $L^2$ loss on node coordinates. However, to keep the training physics-based and unsupervised, we prioritize $L\_{\text{spring}}` which doesn’t require ground-truth next positions, and $L_{\text{SDF}}` which compares implicit outputs to ground truth geometry. For completeness, one could add $L\_{\text{pos}} = \frac{1}{N\_f}\sum\_i |\mathbf{x}\_i - \mathbf{x}\_i^{GT}|^2$ if ground truth $\mathbf{x}\_i^{GT}$ is available for fine nodes.

Our **total loss** is a weighted sum: $L\_{\text{total}} = \lambda\_{\text{spring}} L\_{\text{spring}} + \lambda\_{\text{SDF}} L\_{\text{SDF}}$. During training, we adjust $\lambda$ weights to balance the scale of these losses (for example, $\lambda\_{\text{spring}}$ might be set to ensure the spring loss (typically order of magnitude \~$10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ if using lengths in meters) is comparable to the SDF loss).

We also implement a **curriculum learning strategy** to gradually increase the difficulty of the learning task. At the start of training, the model sees easier scenarios (e.g., static or near-static cloth configurations) and later is exposed to full dynamic scenarios. The curriculum helps the HGNN-NIF model first learn basic cloth configuration and geometry, then progressively learn dynamics and high-frequency details, improving training stability.

One way to implement the curriculum is to split the training data or simulation scenarios into stages:

- **Stage 0: Static drape** – Train on cases where the cloth is static or only affected by gravity (no wind or motion). Here the model mainly learns to represent the cloth shape (via SDF) and respect static stretching constraints.
- **Stage 1: Small motions** – Introduce mild dynamics, e.g., the cloth moves slowly or small oscillations. The model learns to predict small temporal changes and update the implicit field accordingly.
- **Stage 2: Full dynamics** – Include fast motions, large wrinkles, wind forces, collisions, etc. The model now handles the complete problem.

We implement this by adjusting the training loop or data loader over epochs. For example, for the first N epochs we use only a subset of data (or we scale down velocities in the data), then gradually ramp up motion. Below is a pseudocode snippet illustrating curriculum learning integration in the training process:

```python
# Assume we have datasets or data generators for different difficulty levels:
static_dataset = ClothDataset(mode='static')       # mostly static configurations
easy_dyn_dataset = ClothDataset(mode='easy_dyn')   # slow motions
full_dyn_dataset = ClothDataset(mode='full_dyn')   # full dynamic motions

# Training loop with curriculum
num_epochs = 150
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    # Switch dataset based on epoch (curriculum schedule)
    if epoch < 50:
        data_loader = static_dataset.loader(batch_size=4, shuffle=True)
    elif epoch < 100:
        data_loader = easy_dyn_dataset.loader(batch_size=4, shuffle=True)
    else:
        data_loader = full_dyn_dataset.loader(batch_size=4, shuffle=True)
    for batch in data_loader:
        fine_graph, coarse_graph, sdf_grid = batch  # get graphs and SDF ground truth
        output = model(fine_graph, coarse_graph)  # forward pass (no query_points, model returns latent and node updates)
        # Compute spring loss (graph-based)
        pred_positions = output['fine_nodes']  # predicted fine node positions (or displacements)
        fine_edges = fine_graph[1]
        spring_loss = 0.0
        for (u,v) in fine_edges:
            # compute stretch w.rt rest length (assuming rest_length known or initial distance)
            dist = torch.norm(pred_positions[u] - pred_positions[v])
            L0 = torch.norm(fine_graph[0][u] - fine_graph[0][v])  # original length from input positions
            spring_loss += ((dist - L0) / (L0+1e-6))**2
        spring_loss = spring_loss / len(fine_edges)
        # Compute SDF loss
        # Sample points from ground truth SDF volume (or use the whole grid)
        gt_sdf = sdf_grid  # e.g., a tensor of shape (Gx, Gy, Gz)
        # For simplicity, sample a fixed set of voxels (or use all, flatten the grid)
        gt_sdf_vals = gt_sdf.flatten().to(model.device)
        # Create corresponding points coordinates for each voxel index in the grid
        # (We assume we know the grid's world coordinate mapping from dataset)
        coords = generate_grid_points(sdf_grid.shape, bounds=batch['sdf_bounds'])  # returns (M,3) coords
        # Query model's SIREN for these points:
        pred_sdf_vals = model.siren(torch.tensor(coords, device=model.device, dtype=torch.float32), output['latent'])
        # Compute L2 loss
        sdf_loss = F.mse_loss(pred_sdf_vals, gt_sdf_vals)
        # Total loss
        loss = 0.1 * spring_loss + 1.0 * sdf_loss
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
    # Optionally, print loss or adjust curriculum parameters
    if epoch % 10 == 0:
        print(f"Epoch {epoch}: spring_loss={spring_loss.item():.4f}, sdf_loss={sdf_loss.item():.4f}")
```

In this pseudo-code, we simulate the idea of gradually moving from `static_dataset` to `full_dyn_dataset`. In practice, these could be different subsets of a single dataset or the same data with different augmentations (e.g., scaling velocities). We calculate `spring_loss` by iterating edges (this could be vectorized for efficiency). The rest length $L\_0$ is taken as the distance between nodes in the input (which might be the previous frame or the initial shape). For `sdf_loss`, we sample points from the SDF grid; here we flatten the entire grid for simplicity (which is viable for a 32³ grid). We then use the model’s `siren` directly with the latent from `output` (the HGNN part must have been called to produce that latent). We weight the spring loss by 0.1 and SDF loss by 1.0 in this example; these weights should be tuned.

**Curriculum scheduler logic:** The if/elif on `epoch` is a simple scheduler. We could also use a more sophisticated scheduler (for example, monitor performance and advance to the next stage when loss falls below a threshold). The goal is to ensure the model masters easier tasks (like matching static drapes) before tackling complex dynamics. This helps avoid the model getting stuck in poor local minima when training on hard sequences from scratch.

Over training, as the model sees more dynamic data, it will start utilizing the fine graph and the cross-attention more, since only those allow it to capture the detailed wrinkles (which the SDF loss will push it to do). The spring loss component will guide the HGNN to predict physically plausible positions (preventing it from outputting unrealistic cloth stretches just to please the SDF fit).

By the end of training, we expect the HGNN to output a coarse prediction that is physically coherent and a fine correction for local detail, and the SIREN to output a precise implicit surface aligning with the ground truth mesh.

## Step 5: Ablation Testing – Running Experiments for HGNN vs NIF vs Hybrid

To validate the contribution of each component, we set up ablation experiments comparing: **(i)** HGNN-only (graph-based simulator without the NIF decoder), **(ii)** NIF-only (implicit model without the graph hierarchy), and **(iii)** the full HGNN+NIF hybrid model. We evaluate each on a test set of cloth simulations and log metrics like **Chamfer distance** to ground-truth surfaces and **inference time** (e.g. frames per second).

Below is a Python script that runs these comparisons. It assumes we have trained models saved (or we instantiate and load their weights) and a test dataset of cloth simulations (as in HDF5 or a loader). The script runs each model on the test data, computes the Chamfer distance between predicted cloth surface and ground-truth cloth, and measures computation time. It then prints out or saves the results. The code is modular: the evaluation of each model is done in a loop, and we isolate the metric computations for clarity.

```python
import time
import numpy as np

# Helper: Chamfer distance between two point sets (numpy arrays of shape (N,3) and (M,3))
def chamfer_distance(pointsA, pointsB):
    # Compute squared distances from each point in A to all points in B, and vice versa
    # For efficiency, we vectorize using broadcasting
    A = pointsA[:, None, :]  # shape (N,1,3)
    B = pointsB[None, :, :]  # shape (1,M,3)
    diff = A - B  # shape (N, M, 3)
    dist_sq = np.sum(diff**2, axis=2)  # shape (N, M)
    # For each point in A find nearest point in B, and vice versa
    min_A_to_B = np.min(dist_sq, axis=1)  # length N
    min_B_to_A = np.min(dist_sq, axis=0)  # length M
    # Chamfer distance = mean of min distances (can use sum as well, but mean is scale-invariant)
    return np.mean(min_A_to_B) + np.mean(min_B_to_A)

# Load test dataset (assuming similar structure as train but separate instances)
test_data = H5ClothDataset('cloth_test.h5')  # hypothetical dataset class for test data

# Load or initialize models
hgnn_model = HGNN_NIF_ClothModel(node_feat_dim=3, latent_dim=64, hidden_dim=64)        # full model
hgnn_model.load_state_dict(torch.load("model_hybrid.pth"))
hgnn_model.eval()

# For HGNN-only ablation, we use the same HGNN part but without SIREN; we can use HGNN_NIF_ClothModel and ignore SIREN output, or create a separate model
hgnn_only_model = AdaptiveHGNN(in_dim=3, hidden_dim=64)
hgnn_only_model.load_state_dict(torch.load("model_hgnn_only.pth"))
hgnn_only_model.eval()

# For NIF-only ablation, assume a model that directly predicts implicit field from latent without graph. 
# This could be an RNN or MLP that outputs a latent code per time step, which the SIREN uses. 
# For simplicity, let's say we trained a model that takes initial cloth state and outputs a latent code sequence.
nif_only_model = nn.LSTM(input_size=3*init_node_count, hidden_size=64, batch_first=True)  # placeholder
# (In practice, NIF-only might not use LSTM; could be a per-frame latent regression. Here we just illustrate concept.)
# Load weights if available
# nif_only_model.load_state_dict(torch.load("model_nif_only.pth"))
nif_decoder = SIRENDecoder(coord_dim=3, latent_dim=64, hidden_dim=128, hidden_layers=3)
# nif_decoder would be part of nif_only_model; ensure it's loaded/trained accordingly.

# Evaluate each model
models = {
    "HGNN-NIF Hybrid": hgnn_model,
    "HGNN-Only": hgnn_only_model,
    "NIF-Only": (nif_only_model, nif_decoder)
}
results = {}  # to store metrics

for name, model in models.items():
    total_chamfer = 0.0
    total_time = 0.0
    num_samples = len(test_data)
    print(f"Evaluating {name} on {num_samples} samples...")
    for i in range(num_samples):
        sample = test_data[i]
        # sample could contain fine_graph, coarse_graph, ground_truth_points
        fine_graph = (torch.tensor(sample['fine_positions'], dtype=torch.float32),
                      torch.tensor(sample['fine_edges'], dtype=torch.long))
        coarse_graph = (torch.tensor(sample['coarse_positions'], dtype=torch.float32),
                        torch.tensor(sample['coarse_edges'], dtype=torch.long))
        gt_points = sample['ground_truth_surface_points']  # Nx3 numpy array of points on actual cloth surface
        start_time = time.time()
        if name == "HGNN-NIF Hybrid":
            # Run full model: get implicit surface via SIREN by sampling points
            # For evaluation, we might sample points on predicted surface by extracting zero-level set. 
            # Here, we sample the same points as ground truth and get their SDF, then classify which are on surface.
            with torch.no_grad():
                output = model(fine_graph, coarse_graph, query_points=torch.tensor(gt_points, dtype=torch.float32))
                pred_sdf = output['sdf'].numpy()
            # Derive predicted surface points. A simple way: take those gt_points where |pred_sdf| is minimal or crosses zero.
            # (For Chamfer, we ideally get a point cloud of predicted surface. This is complex; one might run marching cubes on the SIREN.)
            # For simplicity, we'll use the fine graph nodes as predicted surface points:
            pred_points = output['fine_nodes'].detach().numpy()
        elif name == "HGNN-Only":
            # Model outputs fine and coarse node updates (or positions). Use fine nodes as predicted surface.
            with torch.no_grad():
                fine_out, coarse_out, _ = model(fine_graph[0], fine_graph[1], coarse_graph[0], coarse_graph[1])
            pred_points = fine_out.detach().numpy()
        elif name == "NIF-Only":
            # Use the NIF-only model to get predicted implicit field.
            # For instance, feed initial state into LSTM to get latent, then decode to surface.
            # (This is pseudocode; actual NIF-only model structure may differ.)
            init_state = fine_graph[0].flatten().unsqueeze(0).unsqueeze(0)  # (batch=1, seq=1, features=3*N)
            with torch.no_grad():
                _, (h_n, _) = model[0](init_state)  # run LSTM to get latent in h_n
                latent = h_n.squeeze(0)  # shape (64,)
                # Sample points on surface: again, we simplify by using ground truth points or some fixed sampling
                pred_sdf = model[1](torch.tensor(gt_points, dtype=torch.float32), latent).numpy()
            # Use an iso-surface extraction or threshold to get points. Simplify: assume output SDF and use a level-set method.
            # Here we'll again just pick some points with small |SDF| or use no predicted points (not realistic).
            pred_points = gt_points.copy()  # (placeholder: in reality, one would find actual predicted surface)
        end_time = time.time()
        total_time += (end_time - start_time)
        # Compute Chamfer distance between predicted and ground truth point clouds
        cd = chamfer_distance(pred_points, gt_points)
        total_chamfer += cd
    avg_time = total_time / num_samples
    avg_chamfer = total_chamfer / num_samples
    results[name] = {"ChamferDist": avg_chamfer, "TimePerSample": avg_time}

# Output the results
for name, metrics in results.items():
    print(f"{name}: Chamfer Distance = {metrics['ChamferDist']:.6f}, Time = {metrics['TimePerSample']:.4f} s/frame")
```

**Notes on the evaluation:** We use `chamfer_distance` as an automated measure of how close the predicted surface is to the ground truth cloth surface. This metric is common for comparing shapes; it effectively measures an average nearest-point error between two point clouds. We sample points on the ground truth surface (in `gt_points` – these could be the original mesh vertices or points densely sampled on the triangles). For the predicted surface, ideally we would extract the zero level-set of the predicted SDF (for the hybrid and NIF models) – e.g., via marching cubes – to get a point cloud of the surface. For simplicity in code, we used the fine graph node positions as the predicted surface for the HGNN and hybrid (which is not entirely accurate for the hybrid, since the SIREN could output a surface that is somewhat offset from the fine nodes). In a real evaluation, one would perform a proper mesh reconstruction from the implicit field or at least take the implicit prediction at the nodes.

The timing uses Python’s `time.time()`. We run each model on each sample and accumulate the time. For a fair FPS comparison, one might also exclude the time of converting data to numpy, etc., but here it’s indicative. The `HGNN-Only` model will likely be fastest (just a few matrix operations on a small graph per frame), the `HGNN-NIF` hybrid a bit slower (it runs the HGNN plus the SIREN queries – though one could also integrate SIREN differently), and the `NIF-Only` might be slow if it involves querying many points or a heavy network per frame.

**Setup instructions:** The script assumes that model weights (`model_hybrid.pth`, `model_hgnn_only.pth`, `model_nif_only.pth`) are available from prior training. You’d run this script after training is complete. Make sure to modify file paths and model initialization to match your actual implementations. The `H5ClothDataset` mentioned is a placeholder for a class that reads the HDF5 and provides test samples (similar to training data structure).

The output printed would look like:

This project fuses the structural logic of graphs with the infinite resolution of implicit fields.

An Adaptive Hierarchical GNN acts as the physics engine, solving the low-frequency lattice dynamics and collision responses. This coarse state is then fed into a Cross-Level Attention Mechanism, which conditions a latent SIREN decoder. The result is a system where the GNN handles the "macro" physics (how the skirt swings), and the NIF reconstructs the "micro" geometry (the fine silk wrinkles), minimizing a multi-task loss that combines edge-spring energy with SDF reconstruction error.

Hybrid Multi-Resolution HGNN → NIF for Physically-Accurate Cloth

**Project title:** "HGNN-NIF-Cloth"

**Mission:** Assemble a hybrid framework that fuses hierarchical graph neural nets (internal lattice dynamics) with a latent-conditioned neural implicit surface decoder.

**Include:**
1. Survey on multi-res GNNs, dynamic graph pooling, and cloth NIFs.
2. Dataset builder: pairs of (multi-level cloth graphs, per-frame node states) + corresponding SDF volumes.
3. Codebase: (a) adaptive HGNN with energy-based refinement controller, (b) cross-level attention, (c) latent-conditioned SIREN decoder.
4. Multi-task physics loss: edge spring error + SDF reconstruction; curriculum learning script.
5. Ablation runner contrasting HGNN-only, NIF-only, and full hybrid.
6. SIGGRAPH-style video generator and comparison charts (FPS, error vs. baselines).
7. One-click training launcher (bash) and Docker for GPU cloud nodes.
